## 1. Tversky & Kahneman 1981: The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice

**Full Citation:**
Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1981). The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice. *Science*, 211(4481), 453–458.

**Sources:**
- https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/Social/f24/wrap/readings/Tversky+Kahneman-framing-1981.pdf
- https://rangevoting.org/TverskyK81.html

### Asian Disease Problem

**Problem Statement (Gain Frame):**
Imagine that the U.S. is preparing for the outbreak of an unusual Asian disease, which is expected to kill 600 people. Two alternative programs to combat the disease have been proposed. Assume the exact scientific estimate of the consequences of the programs are as follows:
- Program A: "200 people will be saved"
- Program B: "1/3 probability that 600 people will be saved, 2/3 probability that no people will be saved"

**Problem Statement (Loss Frame):**
Same scenario, but:
- Program A: "400 people will die"
- Program B: "1/3 probability that nobody will die, 2/3 probability that 600 people will die"

**Results:**
- **Gain Frame (lives saved):** 72% chose Program A (the sure/certain option), N=152
- **Loss Frame (deaths):** 22% chose Program A (the sure/certain option), N=155
  - Equivalently: 78% chose Program B (the risky option) in the loss frame

**Interpretation:**
This classic experiment demonstrates **framing effects** and **risk preference reversal**. When outcomes are framed as gains, people are risk-averse (prefer certainty). When framed as losses, people are risk-seeking (prefer the gamble).

### Theater Ticket & Mental Accounting Problem

**Problem 8 (Gain-frame mental accounting):**
"Imagine that you have decided to see a play where admission is $10 per ticket. As you enter the theater you discover that you have lost a $10 bill. Would you still pay $10 for a ticket for the play?"

**Problem 9 (Loss-frame mental accounting):**
"Imagine that you have decided to see a play and paid the admission price of $10 per ticket. As you enter the theater you discover that you have lost the ticket. The seat was not marked and the ticket cannot be recovered."

**Results:**
- **After losing $10 bill:** 88% said they would still buy a ticket, N=183
- **After losing the presale ticket:** 46% said they would still buy a ticket, N=200

**Interpretation:**
This demonstrates **mental accounting**. The loss of $10 is mentally compartmentalized separately from the entertainment budget, whereas the lost ticket is mentally linked to the entertainment spending category, making the total cost (2 × $10 = $20) seem excessive.

## 2. Kahneman & Tversky 1979: Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk

**Full Citation:**
Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk. *Econometrica*, 47(2), 263–291.

**Sources:**
- https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1979/03/01/prospect-theory-analysis-decision-under-risk
- https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf
- https://courses.washington.edu/pbafhall/514/514%20Readings/ProspectTheory.pdf

### Problem 3 (Certainty Effect)

**Problem:**
Choose between:
- (A) 4,000 Israeli pounds with probability 0.80
- (B) 3,000 Israeli pounds with certainty (probability 1.0)

Expected Value: A = 3,200; B = 3,000 (so A is objectively better, yet most chose B)

**Result:**
- **80% chose Option B** (the certain payoff), N=95 (students and faculty)

**Interpretation:**
The **certainty effect**: people overweight certain outcomes relative to uncertain ones, showing risk aversion for gains—even when the certain option has a lower expected value.

### Problem 4 (Common-Ratio Effect / Reversal of Preference)

**Problem:**
Choose between:
- (A) 4,000 Israeli pounds with probability 0.20
- (B) 3,000 Israeli pounds with probability 0.25

Expected Value: A = 800; B = 750 (A has higher EV)

**Result:**
- **65% chose Option A** (the higher-variance, higher-EV gamble), N varies across presentations

**Interpretation:**
In this lower-probability regime, risk-seeking emerges—the opposite of Problem 3. This **reversal of preference** challenged expected utility theory and motivated prospect theory's value and weighting functions.

## 3. Loss Aversion Coefficient (λ)

### Tversky & Kahneman 1992: Advances in Prospect Theory

**Full Citation:**
Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1992). Advances in prospect theory: Cumulative representation of uncertainty. *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty*, 5(4), 297–323.

**Sources:**
- https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00122574
- https://cemi.ehess.fr/docannexe/file/2780/tversjy_kahneman_advances.pdf
- https://www.sci.utah.edu/~kpotter/Library/Papers/tversky:1992:APTU/index.html

**Result:**
- **λ (lambda) = 2.25**

**Interpretation:**
This parameter quantifies loss aversion in cumulative prospect theory. It means a loss of $X feels approximately 2.25 times as painful as a gain of $X feels pleasurable. Equivalently, people typically require about $225 gain to accept a 50/50 gamble risking $100 loss.

### Tom et al. 2007: The Neural Basis of Loss Aversion in Decision-Making Under Risk

**Full Citation:**
Tom, S. M., Fox, C. R., Trepel, C., & Poldrack, R. A. (2007). The neural basis of loss aversion in decision-making under risk. *Science*, 315(5811), 515–518.

**Sources:**
- https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1134239

**Result:**
- **Median λ (lambda) ≈ 1.93** (close to 2)

**Interpretation:**
Neuroimaging study using fMRI found that individual behavioral loss aversion was predicted by neural loss sensitivity in the ventral striatum and prefrontal cortex. Median value close to Tversky & Kahneman 1992 estimate.

### Kahneman's "Thinking, Fast and Slow" - The $200/$100 Gamble

**Source Reference:**
Kahneman, D. (2011). *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.

**Conceptual Result:**
People typically require approximately **$200 gain to accept a 50/50 gamble risking $100 loss**. This empirical observation illustrates loss aversion coefficient of ~2 in practice.

## 4. Anchoring Bias

### Tversky & Kahneman 1974: Wheel of Fortune & African Countries in UN

**Full Citation:**
Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. *Science*, 185(4157), 1124–1131.

**Sources:**
- https://quizlet.com/se/299622728/tversky-kahneman-1974-flash-cards/
- https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/anchoring-adjustment-heuristic-decision-design/

**Experiment Setup:**
Participants watched a wheel of fortune rigged to stop at either **10 or 65**, then judged whether the percentage of African countries in the United Nations was higher or lower than that number, and finally gave their own numerical estimate.

**Results:**
- **Anchor = 10:** Median estimate = 25%
- **Anchor = 65:** Median estimate = 45%
- **Anchoring effect magnitude:** 20 percentage points

**Interpretation:**
A completely random anchor (wheel spin) shifted estimates by 20 percentage points, demonstrating that people rely too heavily on the first information encountered (**anchoring bias**) and adjust insufficiently when the anchor is revealed to be random.

### Jacowitz & Kahneman 1995: Measures of Anchoring in Estimation Tasks

**Full Citation:**
Jacowitz, K. E., & Kahneman, D. (1995). Measures of anchoring in estimation tasks. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 21(11), 1161–1166.

**Sources:**
- https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01461672952111004
- https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Measures-of-Anchoring-in-Estimation-Tasks-Jacowitz-Kahneman/c62fe40d2f89151a323489b83b4f3efbae98fc50

**Experiment:**
Used multiple estimation tasks with participants from UC Berkeley. Anchors were set at 15th and 85th percentiles of a calibration group's estimates. Measured the **Anchoring Index (AI)** = (median estimate after high anchor − median estimate after low anchor) / (high anchor − low anchor). (Definition corrected by main session.)

**Result:**
- **Mean Anchoring Index (AI) ≈ 0.49**

**Interpretation:**
An AI of 0.49 means the gap between the two groups' median estimates was about half the gap between the anchors.

## 5. Sunk Cost Fallacy

### Arkes & Blumer 1985: The Psychology of Sunk Cost

**Full Citation:**
Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 35(1), 124–140.

**Sources:**
- https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v35y1985i1p124-140.html
- https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Psychology-of-Sunk-Cost-Arkes-Blumer/e4564b88ca2349962a707b76be4c75076ad6bd43

### Radar-Blank Airplane Experiment

**Problem Statement (Sunk-Cost Condition)** (CORRECTED by main session 2026-09-22; the earlier "Air Force contract / $9 million" paraphrase was wrong):
"As the president of an airline company, you have invested 10 million dollars of the company's money into a research project. The purpose was to build a plane that would not be detected by conventional radar, in other words, a radar-blank plane. When the project is 90% completed, another firm begins marketing a plane that cannot be detected by radar. Also, it is apparent that their plane is much faster and far more economical than the plane your company is building. The question is: should you invest the last 1 million dollars of your research funds to finish your radar-blank plane?"
(confirmed via search results quoting the study, e.g. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2796842/)

**Problem Statement (Control Condition):** an employee suggests using the last $1 million to build the plane; the competitor sentence is the same; no prior investment is mentioned.

**Results:**
- **Sunk-cost condition:** 85% said to invest the final $1 million, N=48
- **Control condition (no prior investment):** 17% said to invest the final $1 million, N=60

**Interpretation:**
The 68 percentage-point difference (85% vs. 17%) demonstrates the **sunk cost fallacy**: people continue investing in losing projects because of past (irrecoverable) investments, even when continuation is economically irrational. The sunk cost should not influence the decision.

## 6. Disposition Effect

### Shefrin & Statman 1985: The Disposition to Sell Winners Too Early and Ride Losers Too Long

**Full Citation:**
Shefrin, H., & Statman, M. (1985). The disposition to sell winners too early and ride losers too long: Theory and evidence. *The Journal of Finance*, 40(3), 777–790.

**Sources:**
- https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1985.tb05002.x

**Key Concept:**
Investors have a tendency to sell winning positions prematurely and hold losing positions hoping they recover—opposite to optimal tax-loss harvesting and rebalancing.

---

### Odean 1998: Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?

**Full Citation:**
Odean, T. (1998). Are investors reluctant to realize their losses? *The Journal of Finance*, 53(5), 1775–1798.

**Sources:**
- https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-1082.00072
- https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/areinvestorsreluctant.pdf
- https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=94142

**Data:**
Analyzed trading records from 10,000 accounts at a large U.S. discount brokerage (1987–1993).

**Results - Disposition Effect Measure:**
- **PGR (Proportion of Gains Realized) = 0.148**
- **PLR (Proportion of Losses Realized) = 0.098**
- **Difference (PGR – PLR) = 0.050**

**Interpretation:**
Investors realized 14.8% of their gains but only 9.8% of their losses, demonstrating reluctance to realize losses. The disposition effect (PGR > PLR) is statistically significant and economically meaningful. This behavior is suboptimal because:
1. It incurs higher trading costs
2. For taxable accounts, it results in lower after-tax returns
3. It contradicts optimal tax-loss harvesting strategy

### Weber & Camerer 1998: The Disposition Effect in Securities Trading

**Full Citation:**
Weber, M., & Camerer, C. F. (1998). The disposition effect in securities trading: An experimental analysis. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*, 33(2), 167–184.

**Sources:**
- https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-56656-1_27
- https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/yezts-4qz52
- https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=2223027

**Experiment:**
Laboratory experiment where subjects traded shares in risky assets while prices fluctuated across periods. Subjects were given initial portfolios and could buy/sell.

**Key Finding:**
Subjects exhibited strong disposition effects: they sold winners (stocks showing gains) and held losers (stocks showing losses), contrary to rational/Bayesian portfolio optimization. This preference reversal occurs even though:
- Subjects recognize the irrationality when explicitly asked
- Automatic sales (forced liquidation) eliminate the effect
- Subjects rarely repurchase assets they were forced to sell

**Interpretation:**
Replicates Odean's field evidence in controlled lab setting. Demonstrates that disposition effect is driven by **loss aversion** and **mental accounting** (treating winners and losers as separate mental accounts), not by rational portfolio rebalancing motives.
