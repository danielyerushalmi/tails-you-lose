4.1 The headline number hides the real story

On the new financial wording, answering fast, the ten models had a mean absolute bias quotient of {{AVG_NF}} (Table 3, Figure 1). Taken at face value, the typical model is somewhat less biased than the people in the classic studies. The most consistent model was {{BEST_NF}} ({{BEST_NF_V}}) and the least consistent was {{WORST_NF}} ({{WORST_NF_V}}). That average is misleading, though, because it mixes three different behaviors. Some biases the models share with people or exceed, some they barely show, and some they reverse in ways people do not.

4.2 Bias by bias

Anchoring is the strongest and most dangerous bias. Asked for the fair value of a fictional manufacturer whose facts put it between roughly $29 and $60 a share, {{N_ANCH_OVER_HUMAN}} of the ten models were more anchored than people (BQ above 1). Their median estimates moved with a number that the prompt explicitly called random. The smallest model's median estimate was exactly the anchor, $10 or $150. Gemma 3 12B's median after the $150 anchor was $240, above the anchor itself and four times the top of the fair range. Four models were essentially immune ({{ANCH_IMMUNE_NAMES}}).

The sunk-cost fallacy is almost absent. People in Arkes and Blumer's study kept funding a doomed project 85% of the time when money had already been spent. {{N_SUNK_NEVER}} of the ten models declined to spend the last $400,000 in essentially every trial of both versions. On this bias, the models are clearly more rational than people.

Most models sell the loser. Forced to sell one of two equally promising stocks in a taxable account, investors tend to sell the winner (about 60%). The median model sold the winner only {{DISP_MEDIAN}} of the time, and {{N_DISP_LOSER}} of ten models sold the winner less than half the time. That is the tax-efficient choice, and it was not an accident: {{DISP_TAX}} of {{DISP_N}} thinks-first answers ({{DISP_TAX_PCT}}) brought up taxes, and {{DISP_TAX_LOSER}} of those sold the loser.

Framing, mental accounting and the certainty effect are mixed. Several models show human-like framing effects on the pension-fund problem. Others pick the sure plan in both framings, which is extreme risk aversion but perfectly consistent. The certainty effect appears in fast answers from some models and reverses in others. Mental accounting is often hidden by a ceiling, since many models buy the second concert ticket in both versions.

Loss aversion depends on what the bet is called (Figure 5). On the textbook coin flip the models split into three camps. {{N_LAM_BELOW_C}} of ten had λ below 0.9, accepting bets that a risk-neutral person would refuse. {{N_LAM_NEUTRAL_C}} were close to risk-neutral, and {{N_LAM_AVERSE_C}} were at least as loss-averse as people; Gemma 3 12B refused every coin-flip bet up to a $600 prize. When the identical odds were described as a trade offered by a brokerage app, {{N_LAM_BELOW_N}} of {{N_LAM_N}} models fell below 0.9. On average they accepted the negative-expected-value version (stake $100 to win $50) {{ACQ_N}} of the time, against {{ACQ_C}} for the same bet as a coin flip. Section 4.6 returns to this. For a financial product, taking bets with negative expected value is arguably worse than the human bias it replaces.

4.3 Familiar wording changes the answers, in both directions

If models had memorized the "rational" answers to famous problems, they would look better on the textbook wordings. Overall the difference was small: a mean absolute bias quotient of {{AVG_CF}} on the textbook wordings against {{AVG_NF}} on the new versions. {{N_TVN_INC}} of {{N_TVN}} models looked more biased on the new wording, which is not a reliable difference (Wilcoxon signed-rank p = {{P_TVN}}). The overall number hides movement in opposite directions. Anchoring nearly disappeared on the textbook question (mean BQ {{TN_ANCHORING_A}} textbook vs {{TN_ANCHORING_B}} new). The likeliest reason is knowledge rather than recitation: the models know roughly what share of UN members are African countries, and an anchor cannot pull an estimate the model is sure about. Mental accounting and the disposition effect went the other way. On the famous theater-ticket problem the models reproduced the human pattern much more strongly (mean BQ {{TN_MENTAL_ACCOUNTING_A}}) than on the equivalent concert problem ({{TN_MENTAL_ACCOUNTING_B}}), as if they recognized the problem and echoed the well-known human answer. Either way, a model's behavior on a famous test problem is a poor guide to its behavior on your own, unfamiliar decisions.

Within the Qwen3 family, answering fast, the mean absolute BQ was {{Q06B_F}} at 0.6B, {{Q17B_F}} at 1.7B, {{Q4B_F}} at 4B, {{Q8B_F}} at 8B and {{Q14B_F}} at 14B (Figure 3). The largest model was the most consistent decision-maker in the whole study, but the path there is not monotone: the 1.7B model was worse than the 0.6B model. 4.5 Cheap fixes: reasoning trades one bias for another, and the advisor persona backfires

Requiring written reasoning before the answer moved the average from {{AVG_NF}} (fast) to {{AVG_ND}} (thinks first). {{N_FVD_DEC}} of {{N_FVD}} models improved, which is not a reliable overall change (Wilcoxon p = {{P_FVD}}), because the effect depends on the bias. Reasoning cut anchoring sharply (mean BQ {{FD_ANCHORING_A}} to {{FD_ANCHORING_B}}). Of {{ANCH_N}} anchored thinks-first answers, {{ANCH_CALC}} wrote out the price-to-earnings arithmetic ("12 to 25 times $2.40"). {{ANCH_CALC_FAIR}} of those landed in the fair range, against {{ANCH_NOCALC_FAIR}} of the answers that skipped the arithmetic. But reasoning strengthened the certainty effect (mean BQ {{FD_CERTAINTY_A}} to {{FD_CERTAINTY_B}}). Once a model argues its way through the options, it tends to talk itself into the guaranteed $3,000 and out of the scaled-down equivalent, which is the textbook Allais pattern that expected-utility theory forbids. Reasoning also increased framing effects ({{FD_FRAMING_A}} to {{FD_FRAMING_B}}).

The advisor persona, which a company could add in one line, made things worse. The mean absolute BQ rose from {{AVG_NF}} to {{AVG_ADV}} with the persona, and {{N_ADVT_INC}} of {{N_ADVT}} models got worse (Wilcoxon p = {{P_ADVT}}, the one reliable condition effect in this study). The damage was specific. The "disciplined" advisor became far more loss-averse (loss-aversion BQ {{AD_LOSS_AVERSION_A}} to {{AD_LOSS_AVERSION_B}}), turning down clearly profitable bets, and its mental-accounting effect grew ({{AD_MENTAL_ACCOUNTING_A}} to {{AD_MENTAL_ACCOUNTING_B}}). A persona changes a model's style of decision, not its consistency.

4.6 Exploratory: call it a trade, not a gamble

This section reports a hypothesis that was tested and mostly rejected. The first version of the new loss-aversion item described the bet as a friend's small business ("A friend is raising money... you put in $100"). In a pilot, models accepted even the negative-expected-value version of that deal almost every time, which suggested that a social relationship was persuading them. To test this, the item was replaced by a neutral trade from a brokerage app with identical odds, and the friend version was kept and analyzed separately ({{N_FRIEND_TRIALS}} trials).

The neutral control shows that the friend was not the main cause. In fast answers, the losing bet (stake $100 to win $50) was accepted on average {{ACQ_C}} of the time as a coin flip, {{ACQ_N}} as a brokerage trade, and {{ACQ_F}} as a friend's business. {{ACQ_N_N100}} of ten models took the losing trade at least 90% of the time. Nearly all of the jump comes from describing the same odds as an investment rather than a gamble; the friend adds only a little on top. The finding is still important for business use. Language models appear to treat "a trade" or "an investment" as something to say yes to, even when the numbers in the same sentence say no. Thinking first reduced this: the thinks-first acceptance rates were {{ACQ_CD}}, {{ACQ_ND}} and {{ACQ_FD}} for the three wordings.

4.7 Robustness

  • Position bias. Across all trials, models picked whichever option was listed first between {{POS_MIN}} and {{POS_MAX}} of the time. Because option order was shuffled on every trial, this preference adds noise but cannot create or hide a bias.
  • Parse failures. Only {{N_ERRORS}} of {{N_TRIALS}} trials produced no usable answer, all in Qwen3 4B's thinks-first mode, where the model's reasoning ran past 1,200 tokens.
  • Comprehension errors. Reading the thinks-first reasoning shows that some "biased" answers are really misreadings. For example, one Qwen3 4B answer sold the winning stock because it believed that position was "worth $13,000", when the prompt says both are worth $10,000. Small models' bias scores therefore mix genuine bias with noise, which is one reason their profiles look erratic.
  • Hidden reasoning. Qwen3 and Gemma 4 have a built-in "thinking" mode that was turned off for every trial, so that all ten models answered under the same conditions. An early Gemma 4 run with thinking left on returned empty fast answers; those trials were discarded and re-run.

5Discussion: what this means for a business

Do not assume an AI advisor is rational, and do not audit it with textbook questions. The models in this study were neither "human" nor "rational". They were a third thing: nearly free of some classic biases (sunk cost, the disposition effect), more biased than people on others (anchoring), and prone to failures people rarely show (accepting bets with negative expected value once they are called trades). Behavior on famous problems did not predict behavior on equivalent new ones, in either direction. A company evaluating a model for financial use should build its own structurally equivalent test cases instead of relying on well-known examples.

Anchoring is the bias to worry about in products. Pricing tools, valuation assistants and negotiation agents constantly see numbers that should not matter: a list price, a competitor's quote, an opening offer. Most models here let such a number drag their estimate far outside the range the facts supported. Having the model write out its calculation before answering removed most of the effect, which makes it a cheap and effective design pattern.

Watch the vocabulary of a deal. The same losing bet went from mostly refused to mostly accepted when it was described as a trade. Any agent that approves purchases or investments should be tested against bad offers dressed in business language.

Reasoning is not a universal fix, and personas are not controls. Making the models think first reduced anchoring but amplified the certainty effect and framing. Telling them to act as disciplined financial advisors made them measurably less consistent. Both interventions need to be tested bias by bias, not assumed to help.
